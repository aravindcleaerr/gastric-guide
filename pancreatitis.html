🔥 What Is Pancreatitis?

Pancreatitis is inflammation of the pancreas, a large gland located behind the stomach. The pancreas produces digestive enzymes and hormones (insulin, glucagon) essential for blood sugar regulation. When the pancreas becomes inflamed, its own digestive enzymes can attack and damage pancreatic tissue.

Types of Pancreatitis

TypeDurationCharacteristics
Acute PancreatitisDays to weeksSudden inflammation, often severe, usually resolves completely
Recurrent AcuteMultiple episodesRepeated acute attacks with recovery between episodes
Chronic PancreatitisMonths to yearsProgressive, irreversible damage with ongoing symptoms

⚠️ Causes and Risk Factors

Acute Pancreatitis Causes

  • Gallstones (40%): Stones blocking the pancreatic duct trigger enzyme activation
  • Alcohol (25%): Heavy drinking can trigger acute attacks
  • Hypertriglyceridemia: Very high triglycerides (>1000 mg/dL)
  • ERCP procedures: Post-procedure inflammation
  • Medications: Azathioprine, valproic acid, steroids, certain antibiotics
  • Trauma: Abdominal injury, surgery
  • Infections: Viral (mumps, coxsackie), parasitic
  • Autoimmune: Type 1 autoimmune pancreatitis
  • Genetic: Hereditary pancreatitis (PRSS1, SPINK1 mutations)
  • Idiopathic: Unknown cause (15-25% of cases)

Chronic Pancreatitis Causes

  • Alcohol: Leading cause (70-80% in Western countries)
  • Recurrent acute pancreatitis: Repeated attacks cause cumulative damage
  • Genetic factors: CFTR mutations, hereditary pancreatitis
  • Autoimmune: Type 2 autoimmune pancreatitis
  • Tropical/nutritional: Common in India and other tropical regions
  • Ductal obstruction: Tumors, strictures, pancreas divisum
Indian Context: Tropical pancreatitis is a distinct form seen in India and other tropical countries, often affecting young people and associated with malnutrition, cassava consumption, and genetic factors.

🩺 Symptoms

Acute Pancreatitis

  • Severe upper abdominal pain: Often described as "boring" through to the back
  • Pain characteristics: Constant, worsens after eating, may improve leaning forward
  • Nausea and vomiting: Often persistent and severe
  • Abdominal tenderness: Guarding and rigidity in severe cases
  • Fever: Low-grade to high depending on severity
  • Rapid pulse: Tachycardia from pain and inflammation
  • Jaundice: If bile duct is obstructed

Chronic Pancreatitis

  • Recurrent or constant abdominal pain: May diminish as pancreas "burns out"
  • Weight loss: From malabsorption and fear of eating
  • Steatorrhea: Fatty, foul-smelling stools that float
  • Diabetes: As insulin-producing cells are destroyed
  • Malnutrition: Fat-soluble vitamin deficiencies
  • Nausea: Especially after eating fatty foods
Seek Emergency Care Immediately If You Have:
  • Severe, constant upper abdominal pain
  • Pain radiating to back with nausea/vomiting
  • Abdominal pain with fever and rapid heartbeat
  • Pain so severe you cannot find a comfortable position
  • Signs of shock: confusion, dizziness, rapid breathing

🔬 Diagnosis

Laboratory Tests

  • Serum amylase: Elevated >3x upper limit suggests pancreatitis
  • Serum lipase: More specific than amylase, remains elevated longer
  • Complete blood count: Elevated WBC indicates inflammation
  • Liver function tests: May indicate gallstone cause
  • Triglycerides: Rule out hypertriglyceridemia
  • Calcium: Hypercalcemia can cause pancreatitis
  • Blood glucose: Often elevated in acute attacks

Imaging Studies

CT Scan with Contrast (Gold Standard)

Best for assessing severity, detecting necrosis, fluid collections, and complications. Usually performed 72-96 hours after symptom onset when full extent of damage is visible.

Abdominal Ultrasound

First-line to detect gallstones as cause. Limited visualization of pancreas due to bowel gas. Good for monitoring fluid collections.

MRI/MRCP

Excellent for visualizing pancreatic duct, detecting stones, and evaluating chronic changes. Preferred in pregnancy and contrast allergy.

Endoscopic Ultrasound (EUS)

Most sensitive for detecting small gallstones, chronic pancreatitis changes, and masses. Can perform fine-needle aspiration for diagnosis.

Severity Assessment

Several scoring systems predict severity:

  • BISAP score: Simple bedside assessment
  • Ranson criteria: Calculated at admission and 48 hours
  • APACHE II: ICU severity score
  • CT Severity Index: Based on imaging findings

💊 Treatment

Acute Pancreatitis Management

Supportive Care (Cornerstone of Treatment)

  • IV fluid resuscitation: Aggressive hydration (250-500 mL/hour initially)
  • Pain control: Often requires IV opioids (morphine, hydromorphone)
  • NPO initially: Bowel rest, though early feeding is now preferred
  • Nutrition: Early oral feeding when tolerated (within 24-48 hours for mild cases)
  • Monitoring: Vital signs, urine output, oxygen saturation

Treating the Cause

  • Gallstone pancreatitis: Cholecystectomy (same admission for mild, after recovery for severe)
  • ERCP: If bile duct stone with cholangitis or obstruction
  • Alcohol cessation: Critical for alcohol-induced cases
  • Medication review: Stop offending drugs

Managing Complications

  • Infected necrosis: Antibiotics, possible drainage or necrosectomy
  • Pseudocyst: Usually resolve spontaneously; drain if symptomatic or infected
  • Organ failure: ICU care with support for affected organs

Chronic Pancreatitis Management

  • Pain management: Step-wise approach from NSAIDs to opioids; avoid dependence
  • Pancreatic enzyme replacement: With each meal and snack
  • Diabetes management: Often requires insulin
  • Nutritional support: Small, frequent, low-fat meals; vitamin supplementation
  • Alcohol abstinence: Essential to slow progression
  • Smoking cessation: Smoking accelerates chronic pancreatitis
  • Endoscopic therapy: ERCP for stone removal or stent placement
  • Surgery: For refractory pain or complications (Puestow, Frey procedures)

🥗 Diet for Pancreatitis

During Acute Attack Recovery

  • Begin with clear liquids when pain subsides
  • Progress to low-fat, bland foods
  • Small, frequent meals (6 smaller meals better than 3 large)
  • Avoid fatty, fried, and spicy foods
  • No alcohol whatsoever

Long-Term Diet for Chronic Pancreatitis

Foods to Include

  • Lean proteins: Chicken breast, fish, egg whites, legumes
  • Complex carbohydrates: Whole grains, rice, potatoes
  • Fruits and vegetables: Fresh, steamed, or baked
  • Low-fat dairy: Skim milk, low-fat yogurt
  • Healthy fats in moderation: Olive oil, avocado (if tolerated)

Foods to Avoid

  • Fried foods and fast food
  • Fatty meats: Bacon, sausage, fatty cuts
  • Full-fat dairy products
  • Creamy sauces and gravies
  • Pastries, cakes, and rich desserts
  • Alcohol (complete avoidance)
  • Very high-fiber foods initially (can worsen symptoms)

⚡ Complications

Acute Pancreatitis Complications

  • Pancreatic necrosis: Death of pancreatic tissue (can become infected)
  • Pseudocyst: Fluid collection encased by inflammatory tissue
  • Abscess: Infected necrotic tissue
  • Organ failure: Respiratory, kidney, cardiovascular
  • SIRS/Sepsis: Systemic inflammatory response
  • Splenic vein thrombosis: Can cause gastric varices

Chronic Pancreatitis Complications

  • Exocrine insufficiency: Maldigestion and steatorrhea
  • Endocrine insufficiency: Diabetes (Type 3c)
  • Chronic pain: May require multimodal management
  • Bile duct/duodenal obstruction: From inflammatory mass
  • Pseudocysts: May cause pain, obstruction, bleeding
  • Pancreatic cancer: Increased risk (hereditary pancreatitis especially)
  • Narcotic addiction: From chronic pain management

🛡️ Prevention

  • Limit alcohol intake or abstain completely
  • Maintain healthy weight to prevent gallstones
  • Eat a balanced, low-fat diet
  • Manage triglycerides if elevated (diet, medication)
  • Don't smoke - smoking increases pancreatitis risk
  • Treat gallstones if symptomatic
  • Stay hydrated
  • Be cautious with medications known to cause pancreatitis
  • Manage underlying conditions (hypercalcemia, autoimmune diseases)

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Can I ever drink alcohol again after pancreatitis?

If alcohol caused your pancreatitis, you should never drink again - even small amounts can trigger recurrence. For pancreatitis from other causes, complete abstinence is still strongly recommended as alcohol stresses the pancreas and increases risk of complications. There is no "safe" amount.

How long does recovery from acute pancreatitis take?

Mild acute pancreatitis typically resolves in 1-2 weeks with supportive care. Severe pancreatitis with complications may require weeks to months of hospitalization and recovery. Most people can return to a normal diet gradually over several weeks, though some restrictions may be long-term.

Will I need to take enzymes forever?

Pancreatic enzyme replacement is typically lifelong for chronic pancreatitis with exocrine insufficiency. The dose may need adjustment based on symptoms and stool consistency. After acute pancreatitis, enzymes are usually not needed unless significant pancreatic damage occurred.

Can pancreatitis lead to diabetes?

Yes, chronic pancreatitis can destroy insulin-producing beta cells, causing "Type 3c diabetes." This occurs in about 30-50% of chronic pancreatitis patients over time. It often requires insulin and is complicated by the lack of glucagon (which normally prevents low blood sugar), making management challenging.

Is pancreatitis life-threatening?

Severe acute pancreatitis can be life-threatening, with mortality rates of 10-30% in severe cases with organ failure. Mild pancreatitis (80% of cases) rarely causes death. Chronic pancreatitis reduces life expectancy mainly due to continued alcohol use, smoking, and diabetes complications rather than the pancreatitis itself.
